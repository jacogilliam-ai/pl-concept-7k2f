What stops a send

Both flows run over the same contact record, so the rules that cancel a send are shared. This is the half of lifecycle work that never appears in a flow diagram and is the reason a sales team will tolerate one running.

signalwhat happens
They reply to anything Every queued send in both flows cancels. A person answered, so the software has nothing useful left to say.
Somebody at Payload emails them Suppressed for 14 days from that send, renewed by each new one. Marketing does not talk over a live human conversation, and a rep should never have to ask the CRM for permission to email a prospect.
An open opportunity on the record Suppressed entirely until it closes. Sales owns the relationship at that point and a nurture email reads as the left hand not knowing.
They are in both flows at once The demo flow wins and the model flow exits silently. Somebody who just booked a call does not need an email asking whether their spreadsheet arrived.
A meeting is already booked Nothing that asks them to book can send. Path C's reminder is the obvious one, and it is the single most common automation embarrassment there is.
Unsubscribe or a hard bounce Suppressed permanently, both flows, immediately. The bounce also joins the deliverability report grouped by recipient domain.

Suppression is checked when the email sends, not when the person enrols. Enrolment is a snapshot and sending is the moment that matters, so a contact who was clean on Monday and got a call on Wednesday is still caught on Thursday. Getting this backwards is why a prospect who spoke to a human on Tuesday still receives "still want the thirty minutes?" on Friday.

Where it would actually live. Payload's sending domain already authorises HubSpot alongside SendGrid, Zendesk and Google Workspace, so the contact record is the natural place for these as properties, with the transactional sends staying on their own rail. Nothing here needs a new tool. It needs the send step to read five properties before it fires: last reply, last human email, open opportunity, upcoming meeting, and email status.

Start with the demo page and click the button in the bottom right. It turns on an annotation layer that explains what each element is doing and which teardown finding it answers. If you only read one page, read 03 or 05. Those are the two that are actually about email.

Every finding here came from the outside. Nobody at Payload was asked anything, no system was seen, and nothing in it describes how Payload actually works internally. The findings are things anyone can reproduce: clicking five buttons and watching the address bar, requesting a few URLs, reading a DNS record, and sorting a public article list. Where a flow names a person or a team it says "whoever owns" on purpose, because the org chart is not knowable from out here and guessing at it would be the fastest way to be wrong.
