The path that never sees a calendar. One email, and it has to carry everything a call would have, or the gate is just a rejection with extra steps.

Who lands here
Pre-launch and still deciding, together. Either one alone still gets the calendar.
What the email has to do
Name the two decisions that come before a demo is useful, hand over sandbox credentials anyway, and offer to book manually once they answer. A gate that gives nothing back is just a no.
And it is not one email
The first decision is a different question per industry. Title companies get asked which side of the close they start on, legal gets asked whether trust funds touch their balance, platforms get asked who holds the funds. Five versions, same shell, because a generic "what to decide" email is worse than no email.
Why only one
They did not ask for a sequence and they are not ready for one. The email says it is the only one until they reply, and that is kept.

What never happens

  • No re entry. Once a record closes, this flow cannot pick it up again.
  • No win back campaign, no reactivation series, no "just checking in" six weeks later.
  • No email after someone says no. One word ends it, on every path.
  • No third automated email to anyone, ever. Two is the ceiling on path C, one on path D.

Same template, routed. The form asks two questions and both are a single click. They come straight off payload.com/solutions, so the answers map onto pages and content that already exist.

Step one, one click

What does your company do

The five options are Payload's own solutions nav: real estate, legal payments, insurance, professional services, software platform. Decides the tailored paragraph and the reading. Fires form_step_complete before a character is typed, so an abandon later still reports the industry.

Step two, one click

What are you building

Inbound, outbound, multi party, or still deciding. Decides which engineer takes the call and what the agenda leads with. Asking beats inferring from the email domain, which guesses wrong on every gmail.com and every holding company.

Same email, same code path. Emails 03 and 07 branch, so the generator writes four variants of each. Emails 01, 02, 04, 05 and 06 are byte identical across every industry, so it writes none. Eight variant files rather than thirty five.

Path A compresses when the call is soon. A sequence built for a meeting nine days out is wrong for a meeting tomorrow. Only the booked path has this problem, so only the booked path has this rule.

When the meeting sitsWhat sendsWhy
Three days out or more All four. Confirmation now, reminder at T-24h, estimate, close. Long enough for the booking to fall out of memory, so the reminder earns its place.
Under three days Confirmation now, carrying the agenda. Reminder only if T-24h is still more than six hours away. Two emails inside 48 hours about the same meeting reads as nagging, not service.
Under 24 hours Confirmation only. It is the reminder. Sending a "tomorrow" email about a meeting three hours from now is how automation announces itself.

Booking is also capped at five business days out, soonest first. No show rate climbs with lead time, so the cheapest way to improve attendance is to stop offering dates three weeks away.

Why it is built this way. Six decisions, and the reasoning behind each one. This is the part worth arguing about, because the copy can change and the structure cannot.

01

Booking happens on the page, not in an email

Every email you make somebody open is a place to lose them, and intent is highest in the seconds right after they submit. Moving the calendar onto the thank you page acts on that peak instead of posting a letter to it. Email then becomes the safety net underneath, which is a job it is good at.

02

The qualifier costs one click and comes first

Typing is the expensive part of a form, so the first screen asks for none. One click is close to the lowest activation energy a page can ask for, and once someone has answered anything, finishing feels less like a decision and more like following through. It also means an abandon at screen four still tells us who they were.

03

The exit is named out loud

Email 05 offers three answers and says the third one, no, happens by default. People reply when refusing is easy and costs them nothing. A message that only has room for yes usually gets silence, and silence is the one answer you cannot act on.

04

Proof lands after the commitment, not before

The Fifth Third quote sits in the confirmation email rather than on the landing page. The wobbliest moment is just after someone commits, not just before, so that is where reassurance is worth the space.

05

The calendar refuses dates that are too far out

The longer the gap between booking and the call, the more chances there are to forget it, double book it, or stop caring. So the picker caps at five business days and shows the soonest first. Saying that on the page turns a constraint into a reason to trust the person on the other end.

06

Every promise the page makes is one the flow can keep

The page says there is no drip, so there is no reactivation program, no win back, and two automated emails is the ceiling. A promise that is quietly broken later is worse than never making it, and the only way to keep this one is to build nothing that could break it.
